Can Laurelhurst fill its middle grades?

PSU forecasts Laurelhurst’s enrollment to slip, but its neighborhood is changing. New affordable housing will bring middle schoolers in the next few years, and older homeowners will release family houses over the next two decades. Whether those families stay in PPS depends on keeping their kids close to home: moving 6th grade out of the neighborhood gives them a reason to leave.

PSU forecasts a steep decline, but for 30 years Laurelhurst’s neighborhood has held to its historical norm for school-age kids, while Portland’s share fell 28%.

  • Portland: the census tracts inside Portland’s 1940 city limits (tracts 1–60), the close-in neighborhoods on both sides of the river. Portland has since annexed large areas around it, especially East Portland in the 1980s and 1990s, so the city’s total would describe a different place in each decade. Holding the 1940 boundary keeps the comparison consistent.
  • Laurelhurst: census tract 19, whose boundary hasn’t changed since 1940. It closely matches the Laurelhurst neighborhood, which overlaps but isn’t exactly the same as Laurelhurst School’s attendance area.
  • Norm: Laurelhurst’s average across the six census years outside the baby boom (1940, 1950, and 1990–2020).
  • Who’s counted: every resident ages 5–17, including high schoolers and students in private school.
  • A neighborhood that keeps its families: in 2020, 18.0% of tract 19 residents were school-age, 2nd of 57 older-Portland tracts (median 11.2%). From 1990 to 2020, Laurelhurst’s kid share held at 17–18%, while the rest of Portland’s (1940 city limits) fell 28%, from 15.1% to 10.9%.
  • Where enrollment stands: Laurelhurst’s younger classes are smaller than its older ones: 42 kindergartners vs. 88 8th graders in 2025-26. PSU forecasts the K-8 falling from 574 students in 2026-27 to 471 by 2035-36. As a K-8, Laurelhurst stays above PPS’s 500-student minimum through 2032-33. As a K-5, it would open with about 370 students in 2027-28, but it would fall below PPS’s 360-student threshold for a K-5 five years sooner, dropping to about 350 in 2028-29.
  • The pandemic birth dip: this year’s kindergartners (2026-27) were born between September 2020 and August 2021, during the pandemic birth dip. U.S. births fell sharply about nine months after the first lockdowns, down 7.7% in December 2020 and 9.4% in January 2021, then nearly recovered by spring. That one-time drop makes this single class smaller than it would otherwise be, on top of Portland’s longer decline in births. It will move through Laurelhurst as one small class, reaching 6th–8th grade from 2032-33 to 2034-35, the same years PSU’s forecast dips below 500.
  • hollywoodHUB: the jump to 607 in 2027-28 is the new affordable building opening in Laurelhurst’s boundary. See the Short term tab.

hollywoodHUB opens in spring 2027 with 126 family-sized affordable apartments inside Laurelhurst’s boundary. Its children are the students most harmed by a move to middle school, but who tend to thrive at Laurelhurst.

What’s coming

  • hollywoodHUB, at 4110 NE Halsey St., has 222 affordable apartments: 94 with two bedrooms and 32 with three. 71 are for households earning up to 30% of area median income, and 151 for those earning up to 60%.1
  • PSU expects the building to add about 30–39 K–5 students and 12–16 middle graders in its first four years. Laurelhurst has about 350 K–5 students and 226 middle graders in 2026-27.
  • PSU’s enrollment forecast already counts the building. That’s why Laurelhurst’s forecast rises from 574 in 2026-27 to 607 in 2027-28, the building’s first school year.

Who these families are

  • Nearly every family in the building will earn 60% of area median income or less, so many of their children are likely to be counted as students experiencing poverty.
  • Laurelhurst already serves those students well. Its students in poverty meet the state reading standard at nearly twice the PPS rate (47.1% vs. 24.4%, grades 3–8, 2024-25). See test scores →
  • A walk along the freeway: hollywoodHUB is a 0.3-mile walk to Laurelhurst, across a protected pedestrian bridge over I-84. Mt Tabor Middle School is a 1.4-mile walk, nearly half of it alongside I-84 on NE Irving St., or a 1.6-mile drive.2 That walk falls just inside PPS’s 1.5-mile limit for middle-school busing, so these students may not get a school bus unless PPS rules the route hazardous. Their fallback is a TriMet ride or a car, and families earning 60% of area median income or less may not have a car or the work flexibility to drive.
  • Research finds the move to a separate middle school in 6th grade hurts students who are already behind the most. In a Harvard study, their math losses were more than twice as large as their classmates’. See the research →

Converting Laurelhurst to a K-5 would send the neighborhood’s newest, lower-income families through a move to middle school just as they arrive, the transition research says hurts their children most.

Laurelhurst has more boomers than school-age kids; its share of residents 65+ is 42% higher than Portland’s. As empty nesters’ homes turn over, new families move in.

A generation reaching retirement

  • In 2020, for the first time, Laurelhurst’s residents 65 and older outnumbered its school-age kids, 949 to 922. That group nearly doubled in ten years, from 559 in 2010.
  • 18.5% of Laurelhurst’s residents were 65 or older in 2020, compared with 13.0% across Portland (1940 city limits).
  • About 1,270 baby boomers, ages 55 to 74, lived in the neighborhood in 2020.

When those homes turn over

  • In 2020, 318 Laurelhurst residents were already 75 or older. Another 631 (aged 65–74 in 2020) will turn 75 by 2030 and 636 more people (aged 55–64 in 2020) will turn 75 by 2040, after PSU’s forecast ends.
  • Nationally, Realtor.com projects older owners will free up 13.9 million homes from 2026 to 2036, about 74% more than in the decade before. Baby boomers become the largest source around 2029, as the oldest of them reach their 80s.3
  • In Laurelhurst, that wave builds through the 2030s and peaks in the 2040s. Most of it falls after PSU’s enrollment forecast ends in 2035-36, so the forecast doesn’t capture it.

  • About half of Laurelhurst’s owned homes: together that’s 837 of the neighborhood’s 1,634 owner-occupied homes (51%) with a homeowner 55 or older in 2020, compared with 45% across Portland (1940 city limits).4

Families are already moving in

  • The number of school-age kids living in Laurelhurst rose from 795 in 2000 to 922 in 2020, up 16%, even as the neighborhood’s older population grew.
  • My family bought our house in Laurelhurst five years ago, as part of this wave of new families. Since then, families with children have bought nearly every house that has come up for sale in the neighborhood. Laurelhurst is getting younger.

Laurelhurst’s biggest wave of home turnover is just beginning, and most of it comes after PSU’s forecast ends. Converting the K-8 now would plan for today’s dip, not for the families who will buy those homes.

How the numbers were calculated

  • Laurelhurst’s norm (17%): the average school-age share across the six census years outside the baby boom (1940, 1950, and 1990–2020).
  • Laurelhurst as a K-5: PSU’s forecast for the whole K-8, minus the expected 6th–8th graders. Those are the current 5th–7th graders moving up a grade, plus PSU’s estimate of middle graders from hollywoodHUB. This is my estimate, not PPS’s.
  • hollywoodHUB’s share of new students: PSU’s per-unit rates for affordable apartments with two or more bedrooms, times the building’s 126 family-sized units, compared with Laurelhurst’s 2026-27 enrollment by grade band (about 350 in K–5 and 226 in grades 6–8).
  • Turning 75: 2020 census age groups, for residents and for homeowners, moved forward ten years at a time, assuming everyone ages in place. It’s a guide to timing, not a prediction for any one home.
