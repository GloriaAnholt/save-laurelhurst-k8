What would Rightsizing cost that PPS isn’t counting?

PPS’s Rightsizing plans count what closing and converting schools would save, but not everything they would cost. Some of those costs, like new bus routes, can be estimated from PPS’s own budget. Others, like what happens to the schools PPS closes, PPS hasn’t said.

PPS’s Rightsizing plans don’t say how students would get to their new schools. About 29% of Laurelhurst’s attendance area is more than 1.5 miles from Mt Tabor, the distance at which PPS buses middle schoolers. Here’s what that would cost, using PPS’s own budget.

How many new bus routes will PPS need to cover the 7,000–8,000 K–8 students who change schools under Rightsizing?3

  • About 100–170 new routes if every single student who changes schools under Rightsizing needed a bus, at 48–72 students per bus.4 That would cost PPS about $3.7M–$6.3M a year. That’s an (unrealistic) upper bound; many students won’t need one.
  • About 30–50 new routes if 29% of them needed a bus, as Laurelhurst’s area would. That would cost PPS about $1.1M–$1.8M a year.

How the numbers were calculated

  • The bus budget: PPS budgets $28.8 million in 2026-27 for contracted yellow-bus service (budget line 533110). That excludes taxis ($2.9M), TriMet passes ($2.1M), and PPS’s own transportation staff. All student transportation together is $48.9 million in 2026-27, up from $44.1 million the year before, so busing was not cut in this budget.
  • Counting routes: PPS posts bus schedules school by school. I counted the route files for all 65 schools listed: 223 morning routes and 226 afternoon routes. Laurelhurst has none. Mt Tabor has five. This counts route listings, not buses. One bus often serves several schools and shows up in each school’s list, and a 2020 PPS staff report counted 131 big-bus routes. If there are fewer buses than listings, each one costs more than $128,000, and my estimate is low.
  • What PPS pays after the state: Oregon reimburses districts 70%, 80%, or 90% of approved bus costs, depending on how a district’s cost per student ranks statewide (ORS 327.013). PPS ranks 59th of 100, below the 80% cutoff at rank 80, so it gets 70% back and pays 30% itself.
  • Who would ride: Oregon’s rule and PPS’s guidelines call for busing middle schoolers who live 1.5 miles or more from school, measured along streets and paths. Measured that way, about 29% of Laurelhurst’s attendance area is more than 1.5 miles from Mt Tabor. In a straight line, only about 2% is. PPS hasn’t published a transportation plan for this change, so I assumed one or two routes.

Limitations and context

  • Hazard routes. Students who qualify under PPS’s hazard rules (streets over 20 mph with no sidewalk, or wide or fast streets without crossing controls) can ride even if they live closer than 1.5 miles. This estimate doesn’t count them.
  • Families who drive. Some families will drive instead, adding traffic at Mt Tabor at drop-off.
  • Ride time. A route that picks up across a large area is longer than the straight-line distance suggests.

In the last 25 years, PPS has never closed more than four schools in a single year.1 Now it wants to close 11 to 14 at once, most by fall 2027. When PPS hasn’t found a new use for a closed school, it has sat empty for an average of 17.2 years (and counting).2

Empty schools don’t stop costing PPS money

In September 2026, a School Board member asked district staff how many empty buildings PPS owns and what they cost to maintain each year. Staff answered that PPS has only two vacant buildings, Kenton and Smith. Kenton is leased short-term and is being considered as a temporary home for students during seismic retrofits. Smith, however, costs about $70,000 a year, including maintenance and insurance.4 If Smith’s costs have held roughly steady, its 21 empty years have cost PPS about $1.5 million in today’s dollars.5

But that isn’t even the full picture: PPS’s own audited financial report lists 1,156,926 square feet of “other or vacant facilities,” about 11% of the district’s 10.3 million square feet.6 And PPS has already said it has no plan yet for any school Rightsizing would close.4

If the 11 to 14 schools Rightsizing closes sat empty like Smith, vacant buildings could cost PPS about $840,000 to $1.05 million a year, for the next decade or two.7

PPS’s closed schools have sat empty for years, sometimes decades. Closing 11 to 14 more without a plan could leave PPS paying up to $1 million a year for nothing, money that belongs in classrooms.
