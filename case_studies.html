What happened to the K-8s PPS already converted?

Since 2016, PPS has converted 14 of its K-8s to K-5s. Today, five of these schools are on its closure list in both plans. History shows what PPS’s current plans could mean for the seven K-8s it wants to convert now: strong K-8s risk losing what works, and vulnerable ones risk being set up to close.

Portland has changed its mind about K-8s before. Each time, PPS made sweeping changes all at once, instead of going school by school and planning how each change would work.

1970s

The introduction of middle schools

Historically, most of Portland’s neighborhood schools were founded as K-8 grammar schools, and remained that way through the 1960s.2 In the 1970s, PPS’s “Schools for the Seventies” plan set out to move 6th through 8th graders into new middle schools, partly to help desegregate schools and partly to create better resourced schools.3 The plan involved converting about 20 elementary schools into middle schools; by 1975 six schools had been converted, and by 1979 the total was 12, with two more planned.4

In Northeast Portland, schools including Vernon, Sabin, and Irvington gave up their upper grades to become early childhood centers (gaining pre-K instead), and Black students in the upper grades were bused out of their neighborhoods.5 The plan was sharply criticized for putting that burden on Black children, and it was never finished: voters rejected some of the funding, and many middle schools weren’t built or converted, leaving middle-school deserts in North and Northeast Portland.6 Ironically, at the time, Irvington was lauded as a desegregation success and model to be used elsewhere7—today it’s slated for closure.

2006–2015

Back to K-8s

Fast forward to the 2000s, and facing declining enrollment, Superintendent Vicki Phillips reversed course on K-8s. In 2006, the School Board approved converting six schools in January and another 17 in May; all together, 23 elementary schools were approved to convert into K-8s. Within two years PPS had closed eight schools, including Kellogg Middle School, and by fall 2009 it ran 32 K-8s, up from 3 in fall 2004.8 Phillips left as Superintendent in July 2007 with many of the conversions still in process, and her successor, Carole Smith, carried on with them. By 2015, PPS ran 29 K-8s in total, and Smith later said the change “happened too quickly without planning.”9 The result was a mishmash: some K-8s were poorly resourced, and some would go on to thrive.10

2016–today

Back to K-5s

Since 2016, PPS has been converting K-8s back into K-5s as it pushes for using middle schools: four reverted in 2016, seven in 2018, and three in 2021, plus Kellogg Middle School reopened in 2021, 14 years after PPS closed it in favor of K-8s.11 Today, both Rightsizing plans would convert seven more K-8s to elementary schools. All together, the recent conversions would change 21 schools away from K-8s.

Converting a school costs not only time, money, and heartache for the families and communities involved (not to mention the teachers and staff); it puts the reconfigured school at risk. Of the 14 schools PPS has converted away from K-8s since 2016, five are now proposed for closure.12

It’s time to tell the district to stop flip-flopping ~20 schools, en masse, without any real plan for how each school benefits from proposed changes.

What PPS expected vs. what actually happened

The 2021 PPS Long-Range Facility Plan notes that “For the 2021–22 school year, Sabin Elementary is forecast at 60% utilization. Utilization is projected to remain steady over the next several years, staying at 60% in 2030–31.”9

Instead, Sabin’s real utilization has dropped to 48%: 270 students in a school with room for 559.10

In both plans, Sabin’s students would be sent to Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. School, which is also under-enrolled: 319 students in 2025–26, below PPS’s 360 K-5 threshold and at half its capacity. MLK Jr. was converted to a K-5 in the same 2018 round as Sabin. Yesterday’s K-8 conversions bring today’s school closures.11

Converting a strong K-8 can make it a vulnerable K-5. Vernon is one of PPS’s most cost-effective K-8s, and its middle graders beat expectations in math more than any other PPS school. Converting it would start it out below PPS’s own K-5 threshold, the same path that put Sabin on the closure list. Rather than simply converting K-8s, PPS should present well-reasoned, school-by-school plans.
