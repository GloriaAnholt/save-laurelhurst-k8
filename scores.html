How do Laurelhurst students do on state tests?

In 2024-25, Laurelhurst students scored above the PPS average in both reading and math in every student group the state reports for the school, including students in poverty and students with disabilities. Its 6th through 8th graders posted the highest reading scores of any PPS neighborhood school serving those grades.

Laurelhurst students outscore the PPS average in every reported group. The gaps are widest for the students PPS most needs to reach: +37 points in reading for Hispanic/Latino students and +23 for students in poverty.

  • PPS average covers grades 3–8 only, the same grades Laurelhurst tests, so high school results don’t pull it down.
  • Groups are sorted by Laurelhurst’s reading score. These are every racial, ethnic, poverty, and disability group the state reports for Laurelhurst; groups with fewer than 10 students tested, including English learners, are suppressed by the state.
  • Asian (14 students) and Black (13 students) groups are small, so a few students can move their rates a lot.

Poverty predicts most of the difference in test scores between schools. Laurelhurst’s 6th through 8th graders read 4.6 points above what its poverty level predicts, with the highest scores of any PPS neighborhood school serving those grades.

  • Neighborhood schools: the only school above Laurelhurst is Winterhaven, which admits by lottery. Just behind Laurelhurst are Odyssey (78.1%), a talented-and-gifted program the state reports under Hayhurst, and Beverly Cleary (78.0%).
  • How “expected” works: across these 26 schools, the share of students in poverty predicts most of the difference in reading scores (89%). The best-fit line is roughly 87% minus 1.25 points for each percentage point of students in poverty. A school’s “expected” score is where it falls on that line; the number beside each row is its actual score minus expected. For example, Laurelhurst has about 11% of students in poverty, so it’s expected to score about 73.6%. It actually scored 78.2%, which is 4.6 points above expected.
  • Limits: for K-8s, the poverty share covers the whole school, including the younger grades. The line accounts only for poverty, not disability rates or lottery admission, which is one reason Winterhaven sits so far above it. It is one year of results (2024-25).
  • This compares different schools in one year, not the same students over time.

Sources and method

  • Oregon Department of Education, Oregon Statewide Assessment System results, 2024-25: school and district files for English language arts and math, all grades and by grade, by student group. “Meeting standards” means scoring at Level 3 or 4.
  • PPS average by group adds the number of PPS students meeting standards in grades 3 through 8 and divides by the number tested in those grades, from the district file.
  • Expected scores come from a straight-line fit of grades 6–8 reading proficiency against each school’s share of tested students experiencing poverty (ODE), across the 26 PPS schools serving grades 6–8. The difference is actual minus expected.
